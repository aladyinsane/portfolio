## Problem

State government agencies considering changes to how they pay healthcare providers
need to know the fiscal impact before committing: a change to a payment methodology
can shift hundreds of millions of dollars across a state's provider network, and
finding that out after the policy takes effect isn't an option.

## Approach

Modeled statewide claims datasets to simulate projected payments under proposed
methodology changes against historical baselines, comparing standard hospital
payment classification systems used across state Medicaid programs, running
case-mix analysis, and supporting rate development for multiple state agencies. Built
claim-line-level cost models using cost-to-charge ratios derived from federal cost
report data, and produced the calculations states are required to submit to CMS (the
federal agency overseeing Medicare and Medicaid) to demonstrate payments stay within
regulatory limits.

## Outcome

Gave state agencies a concrete, claims-level basis for payment policy decisions
instead of applying a change and finding out the fiscal impact afterward: the kind
of analysis that has to be right before a policy goes live, not after.

One representative engagement modeled a proposed change to how a state Medicaid
program pays Medicare crossover claims, limiting payment to the lesser of the
Medicaid fee schedule amount or the Medicare beneficiary cost-sharing liability.
Claims were assigned reimbursement amounts through a hierarchical pricing
methodology and sorted into confidence tiers based on match strength, so
policymakers could see which results rested on the most reliable rate
assignments. The model estimated the policy would reduce annual crossover
expenditures by $46.3 million, with $30.1 million of that impact falling within
the highest-confidence pricing tier. Breaking the results out by beneficiary
category (Qualified Medicare Beneficiaries, Specified Low-Income Medicare
Beneficiaries, and other dual-eligible populations) let the agency evaluate
exemptions for specific groups before committing to a design, rather than
discovering the disparate impact after the policy took effect.
