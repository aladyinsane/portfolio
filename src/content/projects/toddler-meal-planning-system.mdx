## Problem

Feeding a toddler on a plan is a small weekly data problem wearing a domestic disguise.
There are real constraints: an iron target, protein rotation across the week, food safety
recalls that open and close, a four-day clock on anything thawed, a ceiling on how much
Sunday prep is realistic, and a set of hard-won facts about what he will actually eat.
There's also a weekly grocery run, which means that by Tuesday the plan is constrained by
what is physically in the house.

For months this lived in an eight-tab spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet was both the source
of truth and the thing printed for the kitchen. That's the flaw. Every mid-week change ("he
was home for lunch after all," "prep didn't happen Sunday," "we're out of oats") became a
hand-edit against a document that had to stay internally consistent across eight tabs, and
nothing checked whether it still was.

## Approach

Separate the data from the deliverable. One `plan.json` per week is the canonical record,
named by that week's Monday, alongside a permanent library of recipe cards shared across
weeks. Everything else is generated, and nothing generated is ever hand-edited.

Two outputs come off the same data file: a printable kitchen sheet, and a phone page for
mid-week feedback that publishes to one stable URL because it's a home-screen bookmark.
Taps on the phone page (ate, skipped, swapped, plus notes) feed back into the week's
feedback file, which is the input to the next week's build. Generators are Python, standard
library only.

Two independent checks guard two different things, and keeping them separate is the point:

- `checks.py` validates a week's data: day structure, meal composition, iron totals landing
  in range on home-lunch days, protein rotation across the home lunches, every referenced
  recipe actually existing, the Sunday prep ceiling, the four-day thaw rule, and date
  coherence (the week starts on a Monday, prep is the Sunday before, every "Fri Sep 4" in
  free text is a real date that falls where it claims to). The rule is fix the data, not
  the check, unless the check is provably wrong.
- `check_render.py` guards the generators. It renders a frozen fixture and byte-compares
  both outputs against golden files. The fixture ships its own pinned recipe cards, so
  editing the real recipe library never trips it. That separation matters, because a recipe
  edit legitimately rewrites the printed sheet of every past week that referenced it, while
  a generator change silently altering output is a bug.

The plan itself carries its own shock absorbers, which is the design decision I'd defend
hardest. Every prep task has an "if this didn't happen" line and every away day has an "if
he's home instead" lunch, printed on the sheet. Most mid-week wobbles therefore need no
replan at all, because the answer is already on the page in the kitchen. When a real
rearrangement is needed, the rule is replan from today forward only: past days are frozen
history, and remaining days may use only what's already in the house unless a top-up shop
is explicitly approved.

The operating knowledge lives in a Claude Code skill rather than in the code, as a dated
log of what feedback revealed: elaborate breakfasts get skipped, so weekday breakfasts stay
fast and familiar. Morning snacks were skipped six days out of seven on days he was out, so
they're only planned on days he's home. Prep slipped to a Monday once and took out both
Monday breakfast and the morning snack, so Monday's first two slots are now required to be
prep-independent, and a slipped prep day costs nothing.

## Outcome

In weekly use. A build takes a single confirmation (which days he's home for lunch), then
reads last week's feedback and carryover, re-checks current food safety recalls fresh every
week rather than carrying forward last week's findings, writes the new week, runs the
checks, regenerates both outputs, and republishes the phone page to its permanent URL.

The checks have caught real problems before they reached the kitchen, which is the whole
argument for having them: a broken week now fails at generation time instead of at 6pm on a
Wednesday. Carryover and the already-have list mean the grocery list shrinks to what's
genuinely missing rather than restating the pantry.

Two of the sharper lessons came from failures rather than design. Branching broke this repo
in practice: one session built the live week on a branch while another synced feedback to
`main`, so the week actually being cooked existed only on an unmerged branch. For a repo
where the working copy *is* the current week, that split was worse than the risk a pull
request would have caught, so this repo commits straight to `main` as a deliberate,
documented exception, with the automated checks as the safety net instead of review.
Similarly, republishing the phone page by file path quietly created a second page at a new
URL, because the path changes every Monday while the bookmark does not. Both are written
down in the repo as rules with the incident that produced them, which is the only form of
that lesson that survives.
